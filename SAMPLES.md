## 1. Strengthen — Strengthen Causal by Reducing Rivals  ·  difficulty 2  ·  `AR_V1_B1_0002`

> Restaurant critic: As a rule, the more celebrated a chef is, the more lavishly diners praise the meal they are served. To my mind this is only right, and it gives us no reason to grow cynical about whether ordinary diners can tell genuinely fine cooking from the merely mediocre.

Which one of the following, if true, most strongly supports the critic's view?

**(A)** A chef's level of celebrity usually reflects the genuine quality of that chef's cooking.  
**(B)** Some of the most skilled chefs working today are unknown to the dining public.  
**(C)** The more celebrated a chef is, the larger the crowds the restaurant can draw.  
**(D)** Even the most skilled chefs cannot cook equally well on every night.  
**(E)** Celebrated chefs often prepare dishes whose subtleties ordinary diners struggle to appreciate.

**Credited answer: (A)**

### Walkthrough

**Recipe — Strengthen Causal by Reducing Rivals.** The argument's gap: Unless celebrity tracks skill, the extra praise could reflect reputation rather than diners' discernment.

**Why (A) is credited:** Choice A supports the view: if celebrity tracks real skill, then praising the more celebrated chef is praising the more skilled one, so the pattern is no evidence that diners are fooled by fame.

Why each other choice fails:

- **(B) — Direction Failure:** Choice B is tempting because it concerns skill and fame, but it points the other way: skilled yet uncelebrated chefs suggest fame and quality can come apart, so it undercuts rather than supports the critic.
- **(C) — Scope Failure:** Choice C is tempting because it echoes the more-celebrated-more pattern, but it speaks to crowd size, not to whether diners' praise reflects real cooking quality, which is what the view needs.
- **(D) — Scope Failure:** Choice D is tempting as a caveat about skill, but night-to-night variation says nothing about whether celebrity tracks quality, so it leaves the cynicism worry untouched.
- **(E) — Direction Failure:** Choice E is tempting because it links celebrity and diners' judgment, but it implies ordinary diners may miss what celebrated chefs do, cutting against their discernment, so it works against the view.

## 2. Weaken — Weaken by Breaking the Link  ·  difficulty 3  ·  `AR_V1_B1_0003`

> By splicing a gene borrowed from a jellyfish into ornamental orchids, growers have produced orchids that glow softly in the dark. This line of work is pointless, however. However striking the effect, buyers will simply refuse to purchase orchids that carry genes taken from an animal.

Which one of the following, if true, most weakens the argument?

**(A)** The glowing orchids the growers produced have not yet been tested to see how long the glow lasts.  
**(B)** The same splicing technique can make orchids glow using a gene taken from a naturally luminous fungus rather than from any animal.  
**(C)** Buyers of ornamental plants have eagerly embraced other novelties, such as orchids dyed in vivid, unnatural colors.  
**(D)** Any orchid grown from animal genetic material must be labeled as such at the point of sale.  
**(E)** Some prized ornamental orchids are cultivated in greenhouses that stay dark for much of the day.

**Credited answer: (B)**

### Walkthrough

**Recipe — Weaken by Breaking the Link.** The argument's gap: The technique might produce a saleable glowing orchid with no animal gene at all.

**Why (B) is credited:** Choice B weakens the argument: it shows the technique can yield a glowing orchid with no animal gene, so the buyers' objection to animal genes does not render the work pointless.

Why each other choice fails:

- **(A) — Scope Failure:** Choice A is tempting because it raises a doubt about the orchids, but how long the glow lasts does not bear on the claim that consumer refusal makes the work pointless.
- **(C) — Scope Failure:** Choice C is tempting because it suggests buyers welcome novelty, but the stated obstacle is specifically genes taken from an animal, which dyeing does not involve, so it does not meet the argument's reason.
- **(D) — Direction Failure:** Choice D is tempting because it concerns the animal-gene issue, but mandatory labeling would make the very refusal the argument relies on more likely, so it strengthens the pointless verdict rather than weakening it.
- **(E) — Scope Failure:** Choice E is tempting because darkness relates to a glow, but where orchids are grown says nothing about whether consumer refusal makes the research pointless.

## 3. Necessary Assumption — Necessary Assumption  ·  difficulty 4  ·  `AR_V1_B10_0010`

> Many folk tales feature seers who are supposedly unerring, that is, able to name the outcome of any wager before it is settled. But the naming of an outcome is correct only if that outcome comes to pass. Thus the tales themselves often show that their seers are not truly unerring, since some of the outcomes the seers name never come to pass at all.

Which one of the following is an assumption on which the argument depends?

**(A)** It is impossible for anyone to name outcomes correctly with absolute consistency.  
**(B)** Tales that portray seers as unerring often leave unstated whether the outcomes they name come to pass.  
**(C)** When tales portray seers as unerring, the outcomes those seers name are generally central to the plots of the tales.  
**(D)** No folk tale has ever portrayed a truly unerring seer.  
**(E)** A seer is truly unerring only if every outcome that seer names comes to pass.

**Credited answer: (E)**

### Walkthrough

**Recipe — Necessary Assumption.** The argument's gap: Unerringness must demand that all namings be correct.

**Why (E) is credited:** Choice E is the required assumption: the argument moves from some failed namings to the seer's not being unerring, which works only if unerringness demands that all namings be correct.

Why each other choice fails:

- **(A) — Scope Failure:** Choice A is tempting because it concerns perfect naming, but the argument needs a standard for unerringness, not a claim that nobody could meet it.
- **(B) — Scope Failure:** Choice B is tempting because it concerns what tales show, but the argument relies on cases where the naming plainly fails, so this is not required.
- **(C) — Scope Failure:** Choice C is tempting because it makes the namings important, but how central they are to a plot is not needed for the argument to go through.
- **(D) — Scope Failure:** Choice D is tempting because it sounds like the upshot, but the argument concerns the seers in these tales and need not assume so sweeping a claim.
